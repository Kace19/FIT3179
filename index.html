Australia’s Vaping Epidemic

A data-driven look at vaping behaviour and its health implications

Vapes are battery-powered devices that use containers of liquid, or ‘juice,’ which can contain artificial flavours and toxic chemicals. The vape heats this liquid into an aerosol — a fine spray or mist — that you breathe in. The liquid usually includes nicotine, flavourings, and other harmful substances. Vapes can look like cigarettes, pens, or even memory sticks, and are often called electronic cigarettes, e-cigs, personal vaporisers, or vape pens.

The use of vapes has increased rapidly in recent years, particularly among young people. Evidence shows a strong association between vaping and future smoking behaviours. Research indicates that young people who vape, but have never smoked, are more likely to take up tobacco smoking compared to those who have never vaped. As with smoking, being around people who vape means you can also breathe in second-hand aerosols from vapes. Research also shows that many e-cigarette users continue using conventional tobacco products at the same time — known as dual use.

Across all Australian states in 2022–23, the majority of adults had never used e-cigarettes, while only a small proportion reported current use. Current use rates were fairly consistent across states, averaging around 6–8%, with slightly higher use in New South Wales, Victoria, and Queensland.

Are young people struggling to control their e-cigarette use?

Overall, young people were less likely to report being unable to control their e-cigarette use than the rest of the Australian population.

In 2022–2023, among all people currently using e-cigarettes, 29% said they couldn’t stop or cut down on their use even when they wanted to. Among youth, 31% of people aged 14–17 and 32% aged 18–24 reported the same.

Young users reported vaping on average 13–14 days out of the past 30 days. About 1 in 4 (23% of 14–17 year olds, 26% of 18–24 year olds) said they vaped daily—lower than older age groups.

Conclusion: Australia’s Vaping Epidemic and the Growing Youth Concern

Australia’s vaping epidemic highlights a complex public health challenge — one that continues to expand despite growing awareness of its risks. National data show that while the majority of adults have never used e-cigarettes, the overall rate of current use has risen sharply in recent years, especially in younger demographics. This surge has outpaced traditional smoking declines, signaling a shift in how nicotine is consumed.

Among young Australians aged 14–24, vaping has become a widespread habit rather than an experiment. Many report using vapes daily or several times per week, and a significant portion struggle to reduce or quit once they start. The accessibility of flavoured products, peer influence, and the perception of vaping as “less harmful” have accelerated uptake in this age group. These trends raise deep concerns that a new generation may be developing nicotine dependence under the guise of a safer alternative.

Addressing this epidemic requires a multi-layered approach: tighter regulation on vape marketing and sales, more targeted education in schools, and greater support for cessation programs tailored to youth. Without intervention, Australia risks reversing decades of progress in reducing nicotine addiction — trading one tobacco crisis for another.
